One-Page Resume Structure

For a high school student applying to scholarships, your resume should fit on ONE page. Here's the layout:

Header (3 lines)

Your Name | Phone | Email | City, State

Example: Jane Doe | (555) 123-4567 | jane@email.com | Summit, NJ

📚 EDUCATION

Your High School Name, City, State | Graduation: June 2025

Cumulative GPA: X.XX (Weighted: 4.65) | SAT: 1500

AP Scholar with Distinction • 6+ AP Courses (Calc, Physics, CS, etc.)

🎯 RELEVANT COURSEWORK & SKILLS

Math: AP Calculus AB/BC, AP Statistics

Science: AP Physics C, AP Chemistry, AP Biology

CS: AP Computer Science Principles/A (if taken)

Technical Skills: Java, Python, Excel, MATLAB (list what you know)

🏆 LEADERSHIP & INVOLVEMENT (3-5 bullets)

• President, Math Club (2024-2025)

• Volunteer, Local STEM Outreach Program (50+ hours)

• Team Member, Science Olympiad (State Finalist in Engineering)

• Hackathon Participant (AngelHacks 2024, Built app for X)

🌟 HONORS & AWARDS

• AP Scholar with Distinction (2025)

• National Merit Commended Scholar (if applicable)

• Science Olympiad State Medal (if applicable)

✅ Pro Tips

  • ✓ Use 0.75-1" margins and 10-11pt font to fit ONE page
  • ✓ Lead with numbers: "3x PR in Science Olympiad score" instead of "improved in Science Olympiad"
  • ✓ Highlight STEM-specific: For engineering scholarships, emphasize math, science, CS
  • ✓ Action verbs: Led, Built, Designed, Created, Improved, Achieved
  • ✓ No objective statement needed for scholarships (wastes space)

Writing Tips for Engineering Scholarships

✅ DO This

  • ✓ Emphasize STEM coursework – AP Calc, Physics, CS, Chemistry
  • ✓ Show leadership in STEM clubs – Science Olympiad, Math Club, Robotics, Hackathons
  • ✓ Highlight projects & competitions – Science fairs, hackathons, engineering contests
  • ✓ Quantify achievements – "Top 10 statewide" vs "did well"
  • ✓ Mention SAT/AP scores in Education section (they matter for merit scholarships!)
  • ✓ Use active verbs: designed, built, created, led, achieved, improved

❌ DON'T Do This

  • ✗ List sports/arts unless they're exceptional (space is limited)
  • ✗ Include "Objective" statement (wastes space)
  • ✗ Use passive language: "responsible for" vs "led"
  • ✗ Go over ONE page (scholarship reviewers skip it)
  • ✗ Use fancy fonts or colors (stick to black text, clean layout)
  • ✗ List every club – focus on depth, not breadth

📌 FOR ENGINEERING-SPECIFIC SCHOLARSHIPS

Engineering scholarships LOVE seeing:

  • • Hard skills: Programming languages, CAD, MATLAB
  • • Competitions: Science Olympiad, FIRST Robotics, hackathons
  • • Projects: "Built X" or "Designed Y" with measurable impact
  • • STEM rigor: AP Scholar status, high SAT math score
  • • Future-focused: Your goals in engineering field

📝 Letter of Recommendation Guide

×

Who Should Write Your Letters?

✅ BEST CHOICES for Engineering Scholarships

  • Math Teacher (AP Calculus, Pre-Calculus)
    → Can speak to your analytical thinking & problem-solving
  • Science Teacher (Physics, Chemistry, AP Bio)
    → Can speak to lab skills, rigor, & STEM aptitude
  • Computer Science Teacher
    → Can speak to coding ability & technical thinking (HIGHLY valued)
  • Science Olympiad Coach or STEM Club Advisor
    → Can speak to your leadership & passion for engineering

⚠️ LESS IDEAL (but acceptable if necessary)

  • History/English teacher (shows breadth, but less aligned with engineering)
  • Sports coach (shows discipline & teamwork, but not technical skills)
  • Employer/supervisor (shows work ethic, but personal connection matters less)

❌ AVOID

  • ✗ Parent or family friend (perceived as biased)
  • ✗ Someone who doesn't know you well (letter won't be strong)
  • ✗ Peer/classmate (not appropriate for scholarships)

How to Ask for a Letter

Step 1: Ask in Person (Face-to-Face)

Don't email first! Ask during office hours or after class.

What to say:

"Mr./Ms. [Name], I'm applying for engineering scholarships for U-M, and I'd like to ask if you'd be willing to write a letter of recommendation for me. I really valued your [class/club], and I think your perspective would strengthen my application."

Step 2: Give Them Time

✓ Ask at least 2-3 weeks before the deadline

✓ Mention your deadline: "The application is due February 28th"

✓ If they say yes, say thank you immediately

Step 3: Provide Them With INFO

Send an email with:

  • ✓ Your resume (updated)
  • ✓ List of scholarships you're applying for (with deadlines)
  • ✓ Scholarship submission link or form
  • ✓ A reminder of your key achievements in their class
  • ✓ Your contact info & the deadlines again

Step 4: Follow Up

✓ One week before deadline: "Hi Mr./Ms. [Name], just checking in. The SWE scholarship deadline is Feb 28. Let me know if you need anything!"

✓ After they submit: "Thank you so much for the letter! I really appreciate your support."

The Winning Essay Formula

📌 The Golden Rule

Show, don't tell.

Don't say: "I'm passionate about engineering."

DO say: "When I built my first Python program to predict weather patterns, I stayed up until 2 AM debugging. That's when I knew I wanted to be an engineer."

Standard Essay Structure (250-500 words):

PARAGRAPH 1: Hook (2-3 sentences)

Start with a story, question, or moment. Grab attention.

Example: "The day I realized 47 students had submitted the same code in our online Python class, I knew something had to change."

PARAGRAPH 2: Context (3-4 sentences)

Explain the situation, challenge, or opportunity.

Example: "As President of Science Club, I noticed our CS students struggled with original thinking. So I partnered with my computer science teacher to launch 'Innovation Fridays.'"

PARAGRAPH 3: Action (3-5 sentences)

What did you DO? Specific examples, numbers, results.

Example: "I created 4 real-world coding projects. By March, 35 students participated. Two projects won regional hackathons. One student told me, 'I went from copying code to writing my own app.'"

PARAGRAPH 4: Learning (2-3 sentences)

What did you learn? How did this shape you?

Example: "That experience taught me that engineering isn't just about solving problems—it's about empowering others to solve them too. U-M's collaborative approach aligns with my values."

PARAGRAPH 5: Why This Scholarship? (2 sentences) - IF ASKED

How will this award help you? Why do you deserve it?

Example: "This scholarship would allow me to focus on my engineering studies and continue mentoring the next generation of engineers. I'm committed to breaking barriers in STEM."

Common Essay Prompts & How to Answer Them

PROMPT 1: "Tell us about yourself and your passion for engineering."

What they want: Your unique story + why engineering matters to YOU

How to answer:

  • → Tell a specific moment when you fell in love with engineering
  • → What problem do you want to solve?
  • → How does U-M fit your goals?

PROMPT 2: "Describe a challenge you overcame and what it taught you."

What they want: Resilience, problem-solving, growth mindset

How to answer:

  • → Real challenge (not "I got a B+")
  • → What you LEARNED (not just what you overcame)
  • → How it makes you a better engineer/student

PROMPT 3: "How will you contribute to diversity in engineering?"

What they want: Understanding of diversity + your unique perspective

How to answer:

  • → Your background/identity (gender, religion, socioeconomic, first-gen, etc.)
  • → How it shapes your perspective
  • → How you'll bring that perspective to U-M & engineering

🎯 UNIVERSAL TIPS FOR ALL ESSAYS

  • ✓ Be specific. "I coded an app" not "I like computers."
  • ✓ Show impact. "35 students participated" not "I organized a club."
  • ✓ Use your voice. Write like YOU, not like ChatGPT.
  • ✓ Answer the prompt. Don't go off on tangents.
  • ✓ Proofread 3x. Grammar mistakes = instant loss of points.
  • ✓ Stay under word limit. Respect the constraints.

Sample Essay Response

This is a sample answer to: "Tell us about your passion for engineering"

Sample Essay (450 words):

When my little brother asked me to "fix" his robot vacuum, I didn't just repair it—I reprogrammed it. Instead of random patterns, I taught it to clean in efficient, systematic sweeps. Watching it work was my "aha" moment: I could use math and code to solve real problems.

That's when engineering clicked for me. Not as an abstract subject, but as a creative superpower.

In AP Calculus, I realized every problem had a reason: rates of change model traffic flow, derivatives predict economic trends, integrals measure energy. My teacher showed us how calculus runs everything—from bridges to algorithms. In AP Physics, I built a catapult and calculated the exact angle to hit a target. In AP Computer Science, I coded a Python program to analyze weather patterns and predict storms 72 hours in advance.

But my passion for engineering really grew when I became President of Science Club. We were 40 students interested in STEM, but we felt disconnected from real engineering. So I did something bold: I created "Innovation Fridays." Each week, I brought an engineering challenge—design a water filtration system, build a bridge that holds 50 pounds, code a chatbot. We didn't have fancy labs. We used duct tape, PVC pipe, and computers. Thirty-five students joined. Two projects won regional hackathons. One student told me, "I went from hating math to building an app. I'm going to be a software engineer because of you."

That moment showed me something crucial: engineering isn't just about solving problems. It's about inspiring others to solve them too. It's about making STEM accessible, especially to underrepresented voices.

I'm choosing U-M's College of Engineering because of its legacy of innovation and its commitment to collaborative learning. I want to study Computer Engineering, work on AI and sustainable technology, and mentor the next generation—especially young women in STEM. U-M's Engineering Learning Community and Design Innovation programs align perfectly with my goals.

The robot vacuum sits on my desk as a reminder: Every problem is an opportunity. Every solution creates possibilities. Engineering is my way of building a better future—one calculation, one line of code, one mentored student at a time.

📝 Why This Works:

  • ✓ Specific examples: Robot vacuum, hackathons, student impact (numbers matter!)
  • ✓ Shows growth: Started with a repair, ended mentoring 35 students
  • ✓ Connects to scholarship values: Leadership, diversity, innovation
  • ✓ Personal voice: Not formulaic, genuine passion
  • ✓ Ends strong: Clear vision for the future + why U-M fits

🗺️ How to Navigate JCF Scholarships

×

Jewish Community Foundation (Greater MetroWest NJ) Scholarships

STEP 1: Visit the Portal

Go to: jcfsnj.org/scholarships

Click "Apply for Scholarships" or "Scholarship Application Portal"

STEP 2: Browse the Funds

What you'll see: A list of 50+ named scholarship funds

Each fund has:

  • • Fund name (honoring a donor or family)
  • • Award amount ($500-$5,000+)
  • • Criteria ("For Jewish students" / "Attending college" / "Engineering focus")
  • • Essay prompt or requirements

STEP 3: Find Scholarships You Qualify For

Filter or search for scholarships with criteria like:

  • • "For students attending college"
  • • "Jewish students" (you qualify ✓)
  • • "Science/Engineering focus" (some have this)
  • • Geographic location (NJ-based)

STEP 4: Understand the Essay Prompt

Most JCF scholarships ask one of these:

  • ✓ "Tell us about yourself and your academic goals"
  • ✓ "How will you contribute to your community?"
  • ✓ "What does being Jewish mean to you?" (for some funds)
  • ✓ "Describe a challenge you overcame"

Essay length: Usually 250-500 words

STEP 5: Apply to MULTIPLE Funds

KEY STRATEGY: Don't just apply to 1 or 2 scholarships!

Most JCF scholarships have the SAME or SIMILAR essay prompts. You can:

  • ✓ Write ONE strong essay about your goals
  • ✓ Slightly customize it for each fund (add 1-2 personal details)
  • ✓ Apply to 5, 10, or 15+ scholarships!

Why? If you win even 3-4 of them, that's $2,000-$5,000! 🎉

STEP 6: Submit Everything

For each application, upload:

  • ✓ Your essay (250-500 words)
  • ✓ Transcript or proof of enrollment
  • ✓ Resume (optional but recommended)
  • ✓ Letter of recommendation (if required)

💡 Why JCF Is High ROI:

  • ✓ Low competition: Only NJ-based + Jewish students apply (smaller pool than national scholarships)
  • ✓ Multiple funds: Can apply to 10+ different scholarships with similar essays
  • ✓ Reasonable amounts: $500-$2,000 per scholarship adds up fast
  • ✓ Community-driven: Judges know NJ/local Jewish community = your background is valued
